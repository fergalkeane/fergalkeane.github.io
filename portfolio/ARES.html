Overview

Moving procedure authoring out of desktop software and onto the web.

ARES is an AIT (Assembly, Integration and Testing) platform. Subject-matter experts use it to author complex procedures, send them through review until final sign-off, and deploy them for use in spacecraft and satellite builds. Procedures produced in ARES are currently being delivered to the International Space Station.

What it is

A fully online platform covering the whole life of a procedure: Authoring, Review, Deploy.

Who it’s for

Authors: engineers and domain experts who write procedures. Reviewers: the people who check, comment on and sign them off.

Core problem

Authoring relied on desktop software (xMetaL) and a mix of other tools. Review happened around the files rather than inside one system.

Why it matters

These procedures guide real assembly and test work on flight hardware. They need to be correct, reviewed and traceable before anyone uses them.

The Challenge

A desktop workflow, rebuilt for the browser

Procedures had long been written in desktop XML software. The job was to keep the structure and rigour that workflow enforced, while making it accessible in a browser and connecting authoring directly to review.

Content

Procedures are not documents

A procedure is structured content: nested steps, conditions, verifications and references. The editor had to support that structure without asking authors to think in markup.

Legacy

Leaving xMetaL behind

xMetaL needed local installs and licences, and some familiarity with XML. Work moved between people as files, which made review and version tracking harder than it needed to be.

Process

Review until sign-off

Every procedure passes through review before it can be deployed. Status, comments and approvals needed to live with the procedure, not in email threads.

Fidelity

No loss in translation

The underlying schema and validation rules still applied. The web platform had to respect them, while keeping the XML out of the author’s way.

What that asked of the design

  • An editor that reads as a procedure, not a markup tree.
  • Validation shown while authoring, not at the end.
  • Clear status for every procedure as it moves through review.
  • Familiar enough for experienced xMetaL users to switch over.

Design lead, working closely with authors and engineering.

I led the UX and UI: understanding how experts authored and reviewed procedures in the existing tools, mapping the procedure lifecycle, designing the interface and component library, and prototyping for feedback and developer handoff.

I worked alongside the product owner, procedure authors and the development team. Backend architecture and the procedure schema were owned by engineering; my focus was everything the author and reviewer interact with.

Discovery & Insights

Learning how procedures actually get written

I spoke with authors and reviewers and walked through how a procedure moved from first draft to sign-off using the existing tools.

01

Authors are domain experts, not XML specialists.

The value is in their engineering knowledge. Time spent handling markup and tooling was time taken away from the content.

02

Review was disconnected from authoring.

Drafts were passed around for comment, and it wasn’t always clear which version was current or where a procedure sat in the process.

03

Structure errors were found late.

Problems with a procedure’s structure often only surfaced after it had been handed on, adding a round trip back to the author.

Design Strategy

Organise everything around the procedure lifecycle

Every procedure moves through the same three stages. Making those stages the backbone of the product gives authors and reviewers a shared picture of where any procedure stands.

Stage 01

Authoring

Experts write and structure the procedure in the browser, with validation as they go.

Stage 02

Review

Reviewers comment and request changes until the procedure receives final sign-off.

Stage 03

Deploy

Approved procedures are released for use in assembly, integration and testing.

Key Decisions

Four decisions that shaped the product

Organised by the problem each one solves.

Decision 01

Lifecycle status on every procedure

Each procedure shows where it is in Authoring, Review or Deploy. Why: it answers ‘which version is current, and who has it?’ without leaving the platform.

Information ArchitectureStatus

Procedure status across the lifecycle

Decision 02

A structured editor, not an XML editor

Authors work with steps, conditions and verifications as visual blocks, while the platform handles the underlying XML. Why: keeps experts focused on the content.

AuthoringStructured Content

Authoring a procedure in the browser

Decision 03

Validation while you write

Structural issues are flagged inline as the author works. Why: catching problems before review removes a round trip.

ValidationError Prevention

Issues flagged at the point of authoring

Decision 04

Review and sign-off inside the platform

Reviewers comment in context and approve or return a procedure, with a clear record of each decision. Why: keeps the review trail attached to the procedure itself.
